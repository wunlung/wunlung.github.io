As AI lowers the cost of producing knowledge work, our value shifts from creating the first draft to defining the right problem, exercising judgment, and owning the outcome.

That is why I think we have started asking the wrong question.

Instead of asking, “How do I use AI better?”, we should ask:

> If AI can already generate an answer, what unique value do I bring?

The answer is not simply better prompting. It is becoming what I think of as an **AI-native owner**: someone who can direct AI toward a meaningful goal, assess what it produces, and remain accountable for the result.

## Output is becoming cheaper—but outcomes are not

AI can already generate code, reports, analyses, and presentation drafts in seconds. This does not make reliable knowledge work effortless. It makes the initial output easier to produce.

Consider an engineer using AI to build a payment feature. AI may generate the implementation quickly, but it cannot independently guarantee that the feature solves the right customer problem, fits the existing architecture, handles security risks, or behaves correctly in production. The engineer still has to make those judgments—and take responsibility when they are wrong.

As first drafts become less scarce, four human capabilities become more valuable:

- **Problem framing:** Identifying the questions worth solving before rushing toward a solution.
- **Context engineering:** Supplying the information, constraints, and objectives AI needs to perform well.
- **Critical evaluation:** Recognising errors, challenging assumptions, and demanding sufficient evidence before acting.
- **Ownership:** Remaining accountable for the outcome, even when AI performs much of the execution.

Together, these capabilities move us beyond generating outputs and toward delivering results.

## Expertise becomes more important, not less

This shift does not make technical or domain expertise obsolete. I believe it raises the value of both.

AI can produce a plausible answer without understanding all the consequences. Experts are better equipped to recognise subtle errors, ask sharper questions, design robust workflows, and make sound decisions under uncertainty. The stronger the underlying expertise, the more effectively someone can use—and challenge—AI.

The future, then, is not simply humans versus AI. It is experts working with AI to achieve outcomes that neither could reliably deliver alone.

## From using AI to designing AI-enabled systems

For some engineers, that evolution may look like this:

```text
Developer
    ↓
AI Power User
    ↓
AI-Native Owner
    ↓
AI Systems Architect
```

This is not a universal career ladder. It is one way to describe a widening scope of responsibility:

- A **developer** produces an output.
- An **AI power user** produces it faster with AI.
- An **AI-native owner** defines success, directs the work, and owns the result.
- An **AI systems architect** designs repeatable systems in which people and AI work together safely and effectively.

At each stage, the emphasis moves further from individual tasks toward systems, collaboration, measurement, and impact.

## The bar for expertise is rising

AI is not merely changing how quickly we work. It is changing which parts of the work distinguish us.

The people who thrive will not be those who can generate the most output. They will be those who can choose meaningful problems, bring the right context and expertise, build trustworthy systems, and take responsibility for the outcomes those systems create.
